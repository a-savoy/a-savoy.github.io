Andrew Savoy

Neuroscientist · Postdoctoral Fellow

I am a neuroscientist interested in how preferences, recognition abilities, and behavioral predispositions that emerge without explicit learning are represented in the brain—and how experience reshapes them. My work asks whether we can locate these capacities structurally and physiologically before relevant experience occurs, track how they are modified through learning and social interaction, and ultimately understand how they are built during development through genetic and epigenetic processes and how they came to exist over evolutionary time.

I completed my PhD in the Integrative Neuroscience Program at the University of Chicago, where I studied auditory perception and mate choice in female zebra finches using extracellular electrophysiology and behavioral assays in the lab of Daniel Margoliash. As a separate project, I assembled a comprehensive tracer-based projectome of the songbird brain (oscine-net.org). I am now a postdoctoral fellow at the Michigan Neuroscience Institute, University of Michigan, in the lab of Kristina Smiley, where I investigate how neuroendocrine signaling during parental care modulates auditory processing of offspring vocalizations.

A unifying theme across my work is that social-behavioral states—courtship, pair bonding, parenting—are defined by distinct hormonal profiles that systematically re-tune sensory processing. Zebra finches, with their vocal learning, social monogamy, and biparental care, are an ideal model for dissecting these mechanisms.

Research

Studied how female zebra finches perceive and evaluate male song using acute and chronic extracellular recordings during vocalization playback and live social interactions. Developed behavioral assays for mate preference and used machine vision to quantify sex differences in vocal and movement responses. Analyzed hierarchical temporal structure in male song and contributed to methods for automatic song segmentation.

Investigating how prolactin and other neuroendocrine signals associated with parental care modulate auditory processing of offspring vocalizations in songbirds.
